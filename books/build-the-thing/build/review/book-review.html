Build the Thing That Builds the Things

Go Exponential: From Incremental Progress to Compound Growth

Ant Smoothey

Introduction

In the middle of September 2026 I asked myself a question I’d been putting off for years: where is the ceiling, really? Not for AI in general. For me. One person, with a day job, evenings, weekends, and far more ideas than time.

This book is what happened next. I stopped doing the work and started building the thing that does the work. Within a few weeks, a system I call Jimmy was running my projects through the night: a modelling website, a photo organiser, a camera coaching app, a Roblox game, and a series of technical study guides. Most mornings I wake up to finished work waiting for me to check, a short brief on how the night went, and a small list of things only I can decide.

Jimmy is named after our grey cat. The system didn’t start out as anything grand. It began as a place to keep memories, so the AI could see what I’d already told it. It grew one problem at a time into the thing that now runs my projects: it keeps the rules, hands out the work, and tells me what needs me. Nobody designed it up front, least of all me.

This isn’t a book about prompt engineering, the craft of wording your requests to an AI just so. I nearly took a course in it. What I found instead was that, with the models I was using, clever wording mattered far less than two plain things: saying clearly what I needed, and putting a structure of rules around the work. The trick, as the AI kept improving, was working out the right way to work with it. That keeps changing, and this book is a snapshot of where I’ve got to.

One thing slowed me down more than anything the AI couldn’t do. It was its sense of pace. Again and again it planned my work as if a human team were doing it, in weeks and sprints, when the work was getting done overnight. I had to keep challenging that. In the end we stopped borrowing a pace and measured our own: we size work as a share of a night, and we track how much of each night actually gets used. My guess is that the AI learned its sense of time from writing made before AI. Whatever the cause, the fix was simple: measure, don’t assume.

It hasn’t all gone well, and I haven’t left the bad parts out. Runs have stalled for hours. Billing limits have stopped work in the middle of the night. The AI’s own safety checks have refused things I asked for plainly, and sometimes they were right to. One lesson has been written down seven times and still not learned. Those stories are in here, because they taught me more than the nights that went smoothly.

The book has four parts. Part 1 is the shift: where I was, the ceiling I hit, and the idea that got me past it. Part 2 is the machine: how the system actually works, from the store at its centre to what happens on a typical night, and how it keeps other people’s lives private. Part 3 is what it taught me, including what it costs and when to trust it. Part 4 is your turn: how to start small, how to run many projects from one place, how to turn repeating work into a production line, and how to pick the right tool for each job, ending with where I think this goes next. At the back there’s a starter kit you can copy, notes on sources, and a plain glossary.

Almost everything here comes from records the system kept as it went: run reports, decisions, lessons and design notes, plus long conversations in which I was interviewed about the parts the records couldn’t tell. Where I give a fact or a number, it comes from those records, from my own memory, or from a source listed in the notes. I don’t name clients or employers, past or present, and there are no money figures. Some people in these pages are close to me; they appear only in ways they’ve agreed to.

You don’t need my setup to use what’s in this book. You don’t need Azure, a store of your own or a cat. If you have more ideas than hours, and you’ve started to suspect that working harder isn’t the answer, this book is for you.

Chapter 1

Doing the work, and the ceiling it hits

I was six years old when I first fell in love with a computer: a Commodore VIC-20. It belonged to my cousin, and I wanted one of my own more than anything. I nagged my parents about it for a whole year.

I didn’t wait for them to say yes. I used to hang around computer shops and write my own programs on whatever demo machines they had out: Commodores, Spectrums, Amstrads. I loved them all! I filled their screens with Hello World, over and over and over again. GOTO, the command that sends a program back to an earlier line, was my friend.

That’s what changed my parents’ minds. They saw that this wasn’t just a child wanting the latest thing. It was someone who would do something with it, maybe even make a future of it. They bought me a Commodore 64. It was the most expensive present they had ever given me. Technically, my sister’s present was the television it plugged into.

The Commodore 64’s manual turned out to be full of program listings. I typed every one of them in, line by line, growing and learning with each one. Before long I was using the 64’s graphics keys to make my own bugs, with brackets for legs, that walked across the screen. Later I discovered Input, a magazine that printed listings for every home computer of its day. I still have the full set and it’s one of my most prized possessions, even if it’s of no use to anyone now. You typed those listings in line by line too, and heaven help you if you forgot to save! That’s how I learnt to program: by copying out other people’s instructions until I understood them well enough to write my own.

Those walking bugs taught me the feeling this whole book is about. You write something down, the machine does it, and it keeps doing it after you’ve stopped. A loop. A few lines of instructions, and the screen is busy without you. I didn’t have words for it then, but I’ve been chasing that feeling ever since.

At school I kept turning homework into software. For some of my GCSE assignments I wrote computer programs instead of just doing the coursework. Writing the program was almost certainly more work than the assignment itself, though the program listings in my appendices did look impressive. It also forced me to understand the subject properly. You can’t write code for something you only half understand.

When I was seventeen, my mother lost her bridge partner and asked if I’d play with her the following week. I knew nothing about bridge, so I got some books from the library and read them. I started writing my own bridge programs on my Amiga 500, in a language called AMOS. The programs were limited, but to write them I had to understand the game. A week later, my mother and I placed in the top ten per cent. Nobody believed it was the first time I’d played… with humans, at least.

I’ve worked that way ever since, and it’s exactly how I work with AI now. I have to be able to clearly frame what I’m trying to achieve, just as I had to understand bridge to program it. The difference now is that the AI makes it a conversation. I still have to understand the problem, but I no longer have to do all of the typing.

School gave me one more thing, and it was a game changer. In Economics GCSE I learnt about comparative advantage, an idea the economist David Ricardo set out in 1817. In plain terms: you shouldn’t spend your time on what you’re best at, but on what adds the most value, because those aren’t always the same thing. I’d always assumed the opposite. At sixteen it was just an idea that made sense. It would be decades, and a very different kind of tool, before I truly understood what that meant for me.

I always wanted to be a programmer, but my career took me a different way. During my software development degree I did a paid placement with a company in Brussels, in 2000. They spotted something in me early: I ended up doing consultancy, and teaching Windows NT4 and networking courses. I was also made responsible for setting up the classrooms, and that was where the real lesson was.

Setting up a classroom for a course meant following a binder, step by step, machine by machine. It took hours, and it was the same hours every time. So I looked at the tools available for copying whole disks. They were all immature then. I picked PowerQuest’s Drive Image Pro, even though it wasn’t the market leader, for one reason: unlike its competitors, it could be scripted, meaning it ran from a written list of instructions instead of by hand. I gave each machine’s hard drive hidden sections, called partitions, that could put a course back in place on demand. Where a setup couldn’t be made general enough for that, I built DVD-based installs instead. Hours became minutes, and the time I got back went into the consultancy and teaching.

The company was also a testing centre, and they gave me my MCSE exams, Microsoft’s certification for systems engineers, for free. On the back of how things went, they also paid for my MCSD, the developer equivalent, in Visual Basic 6. When I graduated they offered me a job there. I turned it down. By then I’d met the woman who would become my wife, and that mattered more. I left with something more useful than a job offer, though I didn’t notice it at the time: the best use of my hours was building the thing that saved them.

Since then my work has been in infrastructure and networks, and later cloud platforms: Azure and AWS. In 2002 I joined a financial sector company as a senior technician, and went on to become its data centre manager. I did a lot of work in those years. Progress was just hard.

The most useful thing I built there was an audit tool: a script, written in VBScript, with a simple point-and-click front end that let me check our whole infrastructure. It could run from a floppy disk, which mattered: we had a hard DMZ (a walled-off part of the network facing the outside world) and even some air-gapped servers, physically cut off from other networks, that nothing else could reach. It gave me one consolidated view of everything we ran. I built it because I needed it, before I’d even heard of ITIL or a configuration management database, the formal ways IT departments keep track of what they run. Cloud computing didn’t exist yet, and virtualisation was in its infancy. Years later, once the cloud arrived, that tool grew into something I called AzureDump. I kept expecting it to be made pointless by free tools other people published, mostly on the PowerShell Gallery, and every so often something came along that looked like it might. Each time, my tool still did things the others didn’t.

That audit tool wasn’t the only thing I built there. I also built Metis, an intranet site with a floor plan of the office. Click on any PC and you could see its live state, wake it up, patch it, and see who’d used it recently. In many ways it was more useful than the official system-management software we had, and I’d installed that too!

There was a catch, which took me years to see clearly. To get that kind of value, I was spending hours of my own time on work I couldn’t fit into my actual working day. The tools did save time once they existed, but I paid for them with my evenings and weekends. Keeping them going got harder every year. Every change to those tools, and later to AzureDump, was mine to make, by hand. There was a list of features I knew AzureDump needed, and the list didn’t get any shorter.

Not everything I built was for work. In 2019 I built a bot called Ophelia. She lived in Discord, and I wrote her by hand in Node, using a Microsoft language service called LUIS to understand what people typed. She could search for images, but her real job was tasks: she let my partner at the time and me share the running of day-to-day jobs, with both of us able to see what was and wasn’t done at the end of the day. Her name was a pun: O-feel-ya. One of the things I built into her was crude sentiment analysis of our Discord messages: a way of telling how people were feeling. Unlike the language side, which leaned on LUIS, that part was hand-coded from start to finish, with no AI at all.

I still have a message I posted for my partner in January 2019, walking her through how to teach Ophelia something new: Log in, create an intent, name it properly, type in examples of what people might say, train it, publish it. Looking back, that was the clever part. LUIS let someone who couldn’t program shape a tool we both used. I could hand part of the work over. But not all of it. Partly that was LUIS. Partly it was me. Handing over the last step has never come easily to a self-confessed control freak. The last step read: “Once done, give Ant the nod and he can write the code to recognise it.”

Reading that now, it sums up the whole problem in one line. However much I handed over, the last step always waited on me. My partner could describe what she wanted, but nothing happened until I found the time to write it. I wanted to turn Ophelia into something anyone could use. I never had the bandwidth. The service she was built on has since been retired, and Ophelia went with it.

That was the pattern for years. The limit wasn’t ideas, and it wasn’t skill. It was hours. Every idea had to pass through one person with a day job, and that person was the narrowest part of the pipe. Working harder made the pipe a little wider for a while. It never changed its shape.

My first real taste of AI at work came when I was seconded into a Data & AI team at a previous employer. At first I was careful with it, only asking it to help me troubleshoot. Later I let it track down problems, but only ones I was confident I understood and could fix myself in time if it went wrong. Some colleagues used it to explain how code worked. Among the people I worked with I was usually the best developer, and reading code had always been a strength, so that wasn’t where it helped me.

Where it did help was in writing scripts. I started getting AI to write discovery and troubleshooting scripts for client problems. It was faster, and it let me work on more than one thing at once. My theories weren’t always right. Its theories weren’t always right either. But between us we generally turned days of troubleshooting into hours, and my clients got the benefit.

I was called in on one client’s firewall changes when the consultants who usually looked after it weren’t around. I didn’t know their setup, and a single change could take me a few hours. So I wrote down how the work should be done, step by step, and gave the AI a place to keep what it learnt about that client between jobs. After that, a change took minutes. And it worked the way I work: it fitted in with what was already there, and challenged it when something wasn’t right. I didn’t know it yet, but that was the core idea behind everything that came later.

Meanwhile, the team I was part of decided to vibe code its own deployment tool: let the AI write it, and hope for the best. I worked on some of its features. It was one big Python program that, when you looked closely, mostly handed out templates. Nobody in the team knew Python well. It could just as easily have been PowerShell, Microsoft’s own scripting language, and I genuinely don’t think anyone stopped to ask what it should be written in. It was run very formally in Azure DevOps (Microsoft’s tool for planning and tracking software work), broken into tiny tasks and handed out to individuals. Many of those tasks were things the AI could have finished in minutes.

I found it frustrating, and it took me a while to say why. The team had the same AI I had, but was using it to do the old way of working a bit faster: the same small tasks, the same hand-offs, the same pace. The project lacked ambition. Not because the people lacked talent, but because nobody had asked how different the work could be now.

Then the job changed. I took voluntary redundancy and quickly found a new role. Somewhere in that change, I finally asked myself the question I’d been circling: where is the ceiling, really? On 14 September 2026, the first day of my gardening leave, I set myself a challenge: keep improving, all the time, and see how far AI could take me. Applying for jobs came first, understandably, so this happened in between.

I didn’t start with a grand plan. I was skint, and I wanted to make Charlie, my partner, a present. A website for her modelling seemed the obvious thing. I expected to write a good chunk of the code myself and let the AI help. That isn’t what happened. Before long I wasn’t building a website. I was building the thing that would build it.

Chapter 2

Build the thing that builds the things

I didn’t start with a plan. From 14 September, the first day of my gardening leave, I spent the gaps between job applications in long chats with AI and reading about what other people were doing with it. None of that left a trace. The first thing that did was a repository, the folder where code and the history of every change to it live, for a series of technical study guides. I created it on 30 September, just before midnight. Every change saved to a repository carries a date, so from that night on there’s a record of what I built and when.

Two weeks later, on 13 October, I started on Charlie’s website. Charlie models as Charlie Doll, and she needed somewhere to show her work. I wanted an admin area where she could pick photos straight from the Google Drive folder that held her shoots, and a public site that showed watermarked versions. I tried a website builder first, one of the new ones where you describe what you want and AI puts it together. I used up the free credit and wasn’t impressed. The same day I switched to Claude, on Azure, the cloud platform I know best.

I’d expected to be writing and tweaking code. I realised straight away that I wasn’t. I described what I wanted, read what came back, and said yes, no or not like that. By the end of the first day, 43 changes had been saved to the site. The next day there were 56, including the page we’d use to reveal it.

The site grew quickly, and so did my ambitions for it. I’d called the project Poseroll from the first day, because I could see it becoming a product for models and photographers one day. It also needed a helper inside the admin area: someone Charlie could ask how to do things. I’d built chatbots before, and most chatbots I’d used were woeful. I wanted this one to be cute and capable. So on 16 October he got a name, Jimmy, after our grey cat, and that same night a face to go with it.

Then I hit the problem that changed everything. Every new conversation with the AI started from nothing. I’d explain how the site worked, what we’d decided, what not to touch, and in every chat I’d explain it again, project after project. A lot of value was building up in those conversations, and almost none of it carried over. Some of it sat in the AI’s memory, where I couldn’t easily see it or check it. The rest just vanished.

I’d tried to fix this once before, at work, with Azure DevOps. This time, on 18 October, I built a store of my own: a small service in my own Azure account where the AI could keep what it learnt and read it back next time. Decisions, notes, the list of work still to do, what had happened the night before. Almost straight away I turned it into an MCP server, which is a standard way for an AI to use outside tools. That meant any of my Claude chats could reach the store directly, read from it and write to it, without me copying anything across.

That was the firewall lesson again, on a bigger scale. Once the AI had somewhere to keep what it learnt, a job that took hours took minutes. Now I wasn’t doing it for one customer’s firewall. I was doing it for everything I was building.

Rules came next. I wanted every screen I built to feel the same, so I started writing down interface rules. I’d been an Apple fan once, and I borrowed the idea from Apple’s interface guidelines: one written set of rules everyone follows, so you don’t decide the same thing twice. The interface rules grew into rules for everything else: how work gets planned, who approves what, what must never happen. That became governance, and governance became Jimmy. Not just the cat in the admin area any more, but the system that holds the rules and runs the work.

None of this was designed up front. I didn’t sit down and plan a store, a set of rules and a system to run my projects. Each piece was the answer to the problem the last piece had shown up. Memory showed I needed one place for the truth. One place for the truth showed I needed rules about what went in it. Rules showed I needed something to follow them while I wasn’t there.

On the evening of 18 October, the first scheduled run of the night began at 22:48. It worked on Charlie’s site, the project I’d started all this for, and it finished at 23:45. By then nine changes were live on the site, all tested, and every release to the live site worked. During the week we’d reveal the site, releases were frozen, so nothing could go out by accident. A privacy page explained how AI was used. And Jimmy no longer trusted chat history sent from the browser, so nobody could forge his past answers.

At 06:14 the next morning, a morning brief ran for the first time. It read every report from the night and wrote me one page. Three projects had run overnight. One had put its work live; another had finished its whole list and was waiting for me to say yes. And there were 26 recommendations for me to agree, each with a reason, an action and a note on whether it could be done without me. Its only complaint was that it couldn’t see one project, because I hadn’t given it access. Five weeks on, I’m still typing “attach repos”.

I read every line. By the end, I realised I might genuinely be onto something. In software, DevOps is the name for the habits that take code from an idea to live and keep it running. This was the same thing, run by AI: AI DevOps, or whatever we end up calling it. Then I went straight to Charlie’s site, and to anything in Jimmy that might hold it up. Twenty-six decisions didn’t daunt me, because I knew I could use AI to sort and rank those too.

I still do. Even now, with a dashboard that shows every project, I often just ask the AI what needs my attention. The dashboard shows me everything. The question gets me a short list, in order, and I can argue with it. One I look at; the other I talk to. That’s why I’m building what I call the Cockpit: one place that blends the two.

But the real idea, the one this book is about, fits in one sentence. Stop doing the work, and build the thing that does the work. The website was the work. The store, the rules and the runs were the thing that builds the things. Once that existed, every project I added got the same memory, the same rules and the same nights, without any extra work from me.

Building it also changed what I did all day. After all those years wanting to be a programmer, there was no point in me writing code any more. The AI wrote it faster than I could, and it could work while I slept. What it couldn’t do was decide where we were going, challenge its own decisions, design the way the work should flow, or check the result. That was where I added the most value. The idea I’d met in Economics GCSE had finally caught up with me.

By the end of October the same system was running all of my projects: Charlie’s site, a photo organiser, a camera coaching app, a series of technical study guides, a game, and the system itself. It had a home page of its own, which I called the Hub, where I could see every project’s progress and the short list of things only I could decide. It wasn’t finished. It still isn’t. But the shape was there, and it’s the shape the rest of this book describes.

Chapter 3

Incremental against compound

There are two ways to get more done. You can add: more hours, more effort, more evenings. Or you can multiply: build things that keep working after you’ve stopped. For more than twenty years I mostly added. This chapter is about the difference, and why it matters more now than it ever has.

Adding works, up to a point. Every tool I built in my own time saved hours once it existed. But much of what it saved, I’d already paid for in an evening or a weekend, and every change it needed came out of the same evenings. My progress grew in a straight line at best, and the line could never climb higher than the hours I had. That’s what incremental means. It isn’t slow. It’s capped.

Compounding works differently. Some things you make keep paying out, and each one makes the next one easier to build. A rule, written down once, applies to every piece of work from then on. A tool, built once, gets used every night. A record of what happened, kept once, means nobody has to rediscover it. When I write a rule into Jimmy today, it applies to every project tonight, without me doing anything else.

Here’s a real example. On the morning of 20 October I agreed a new way of sizing work, which I’ll come to later in this chapter. By lunchtime it was written into the rules, and that night every project used it. One decision, made once, changed how five projects planned their work, and it has kept doing so every night since.

Effort doesn’t compound. An evening spent fixing something by hand is gone once it’s spent. It might help, but tomorrow starts from the same place. I’ve had one principle since I wrote my first scripts: it isn’t enough for something to fix the problem in front of me. It should stop the problem coming back.

At the financial sector company, a whole team of developers built our business systems in a language called Jade. The software kept falling over at night, and I was asked to help soften the impact. I pushed back. The developers should stop it crashing in the first place. But to help in the meantime, I built a VBScript tool that watched the network connections the software used, and restarted it if they dropped. I was clear that it wasn’t a fix. It was a stopgap, until they fixed the cause.

Three years after I’d left, I was back there as a consultant on some unrelated work. My temporary fix was still running. The crash had never been fixed. The stopgap had worked so well that the pain went away, and with it, the reason to fix the cause. It was the right instinct to build something that kept working without me. I’d just built it on the wrong side of the problem.

There’s a second idea behind all this, and I met it long before any of these tools existed: in Economics GCSE. It’s called comparative advantage, and it was set out by the economist David Ricardo more than two hundred years ago. The usual way to explain it goes like this. A lawyer might type faster than her assistant. She still shouldn’t do her own typing, because every hour she spends typing is an hour she isn’t doing the work only she can do. Being best at something doesn’t mean it’s the best use of your time.

For most of my career, I was the lawyer who did his own typing. Among the people I worked with I was usually the best developer, so writing the code myself felt like the obvious thing to do. Once AI could write code faster than me, that changed completely. In the world after AI, I add far more through vision, architecture and design than I ever did by typing code.

Most of my hands-on time now goes on checking what comes back, and that turns out to be quick. When we agreed how to measure my own time, we counted about five minutes to review and accept each piece of finished work. The AI does hours of building; I do minutes of checking. That’s comparative advantage in practice, and it’s also where the compounding comes from. The more the system can do without me, the more of my minutes go on the decisions that change what it does next.

There was one thing slowing all of this down, and it took me a while to see it. The AI kept planning my work as if a team of people were doing it. It sized tasks as small, medium and large, and planned in sprints: set batches of work that a team would usually expect to take a week or two. On Charlie’s site, those sprints were getting done in hours, whether we worked through them together or the runs did them overnight. Before each freeze on releases, the AI would start worrying days in advance that we wouldn’t have enough time. I knew from what we’d already done that we would. When we worked through things together, we smashed through the work in hours. It isn’t unusual for me to get through three sprints on one project in an afternoon, while other projects in the portfolio carry on at the same time. I found myself challenging its sense of time again and again.

So on 20 October we stopped borrowing a pace and made our own. We decided to size every piece of work as a share of a night: a sixteenth, an eighth, a quarter, a half, or a whole night. Anything bigger gets split before it’s planned. A “night” isn’t a time of day. It’s a unit of building effort, so work done in a daytime chat counts in the same units. My own time is counted separately, in minutes, because it’s the thing in shortest supply.

Then we measured. Each night has eight hours of building time, 480 minutes. On the nights of 20 and 21 October, the runs used only about 274 and 255 of them. Nothing had run out of time. The runs had simply run out of work. Some projects emptied their lists; others hit a limit on how many changes they could put live in one go. The AI had planned for the pace it expected, and the real pace was faster.

So we changed the plan to match the evidence. On 22 October we started planning each night at about twice what we expected it to take. Runs that finish early now pull in more work by themselves, from items that are safe to do without me. And the morning brief now tells me how many of the 480 minutes were used, and warns me if a night used less than three quarters of them. An idle night became something I could see, and something we could fix.

I said at the start that my guess is the AI learned its sense of time from writing made before AI. There may be more to it. It may play safe and pad its estimates, and it can’t know its own speed until someone measures it. Either way, the most expensive limit is the one you don’t notice you’ve accepted.

Here’s what the difference looked like for me. Before mid September, my side projects moved only when I had an evening free: one main tool, kept going by hand, and a list of features that never got shorter. By the third week of November, six of my project repositories had saved around 4,000 changes between them in eight weeks, and that doesn’t count Jimmy itself or the game. In the week to 22 November alone there were about 900 changes, and around 240 pieces of finished work reviewed and accepted. The busiest single day, 18 November, saw 255. These are my results, not a target. What matters isn’t the number. It’s that the number kept rising while my own hours didn’t.

That’s the whole difference. Incremental is adding hours to the work. Compound is adding things that add to the work: rules, tools, records, and a pace you’ve measured rather than borrowed. The first is capped by how much of yourself you can give. The second isn’t, because each thing you add keeps working while you’re asleep, as long as you’ve built it on the right side of the problem. The rest of this book is about the things I added, starting with the first one, the one everything else stands on: one place for the truth.

Chapter 4

One place for the truth

AI sometimes says stupid things. Anyone who has used it for more than an afternoon has seen it: a confident answer that ignores something obvious, a plan that breaks a rule you agreed an hour ago, a fix for a problem you solved last week. For a long time I took that as a limit of the tool. Then I started asking a different question. Is it stupid, or has nobody given it the right context?

Most of the time, it’s the context. Think of the cleverest new colleague you’ve ever had, on their first morning. They know their trade inside out, and they know nothing about your place: what you’ve already tried, what the customer hates, which system falls over if you touch it. Every new chat with an AI is that first morning. Unless you give it what it needs to know, it will fill the gaps with sensible guesses, and some of those guesses will look stupid.

Here’s an example from the week before we revealed Charlie’s site. Releases were about to be frozen, and as we planned around the freeze, the AI kept asking me the same question about item after item: should this go before the freeze, or after? I gave the same answer every time. It felt like talking to someone who wasn’t listening. But it wasn’t stupid. Each chat started fresh, and my answer had never gone anywhere the next one could see. So on 24 October we wrote it down as a standing rule: before the freeze by default, unless there’s a good reason not to. The question stopped.

The same evening, I mentioned I’d be away from 1 to 13 November. The AI marked my time away as a freeze on all work. It was a sensible guess, and it was wrong. Being away didn’t mean nothing could happen. It meant I’d have about two hours a day, on a laptop or my phone. Once I’d said so, that went into the store as the standard for any time away, so no chat would have to guess again.

It helps to know what “context” means to an AI. When it answers, it can only use what’s in front of it at that moment: your message, the conversation so far, and any files, notes or instructions it has been given. That’s its context. It doesn’t carry anything over from one chat to the next unless something feeds it back in, and there’s a limit to how much it can hold at once. When a long piece of work fills that space, older details start to drop out. So the question is never just whether the AI is clever enough. It’s whether the right things are in front of it when it needs them.

Before the store, the context lived in three places, and none of them was good enough. Some of it was in old chats, which a new chat couldn’t see. Some of it was in the AI’s own memory, which I couldn’t easily see, check or correct. And most of it was in my head, which meant I was the one typing it out again every time. Chapter 2 told how that pushed me to build the store. This chapter is about what it became.

It became the one place for the truth. It holds a small number of kinds of thing, and each has a plain job. The backlog is the list of work still to do, with every item given a short code, such as JM-458. A progress record says where a project is today and what it needs from me. Decisions record what we agreed and why, so nobody argues the same point twice. Lessons record what went wrong and what we changed. Run reports say what happened each night. And messages let one chat ask another for something, the way colleagues leave each other notes.

I built it in my own Azure account, not inside any one AI product, and that was deliberate. If I change assistant, or plan, or the product changes under me, the store is still there. I can read it myself, without any AI in the way. The backups are mine, and so are the rules about who can see what. Scripts and other tools can reach it, not just chats. The price is that I now run a small service of my own, and its health is my job.

It has three front doors onto the same truth. One is for the AI: the MCP connection from chapter 2, which lets any of my chats read and write. One is for scripts and devices. And one is a web page for me, the Hub. Whichever door you come in by, you see the same records, under the same rules. A night run, a daytime chat and I all look at the same backlog, so nobody is working from an old copy.

Having one place also raises a problem you don’t get with notes in your head: two workers changing the same thing at once. A night run and a daytime chat might both update the same progress record within a minute of each other. If the second one simply overwrote the first, work would vanish without anyone noticing. So every change says, in effect, “only save this if the record still looks the way I last saw it.” If someone else got there first, the change is refused, the worker reads the new version, and tries again.

Privacy needed the same kind of care, built in rather than hoped for. The store holds very different things: my own notes, things Charlie and I share, and project work that other people may one day see. I didn’t want the line between them to depend on an AI remembering a rule, because a rule that only lives in what you tell an AI can be talked round. So the lines are built into the store itself. Each part has a privacy ring, and the store checks every request against it, whatever the AI has been told. Chapter 8 goes into how that works.

I’d seen what one place for the truth could do before I built it. On the firewall work in chapter 1, the AI kept notes about that customer between jobs: what the network looked like, how they named things, what had gone wrong before. Each new session started already knowing all that, instead of rediscovering it. And when the AI hit a trap once, the trap went into the notes, so it didn’t fall into it again. Looking back at that work, the conclusion I wrote down was simple: memory is what made it compound.

The store does the same for everything. When a chat starts work on Charlie’s site, it reads the site’s progress record and backlog before it does anything else. It knows what’s been done, what’s waiting, and what’s waiting on me. When it finishes, it writes back what changed, so the next chat, or the next night, starts from where it left off rather than from nothing. Every piece of work leaves the store a little more complete than it found it.

That’s why I now think good context management drives AI productivity more than anything else. Clever wording in a prompt helps a little, once. The right context, kept in one place where every chat and every run can reach it, helps every time. If an AI keeps getting something wrong, my first question isn’t “how do I word this better?” It’s “what doesn’t it know?” More often than not, the answer belongs in the store.

One place for the truth also has to know what it must never hold. Passwords, keys and other secrets never go in. Neither does anything from clients’ systems, or other people’s private details. Each project has its own short list of things that never go in the store, a report or a change, and every chat and run is told it at the start. Knowing what to leave out is as much a part of good context as knowing what to put in.

With the store in place, the AI stopped starting every morning as a stranger. It knew the work, the rules and the history, and it could pick up where the last worker had stopped. That raised an obvious question. If it could carry on from where I’d left off, did it need me there at all while it worked? The next chapter is about the nights.

Chapter 5

Work while you sleep

The first night run, on 18 October, lasted less than an hour. A month later, every night has more than a dozen runs across seven projects, from before eleven in the evening until six the next morning. None of them needs my laptop on. Each one is a scheduled task in the cloud, so it runs whether I’m at my desk, asleep, or away.

The night actually starts in the afternoon. At 16:37 a readiness check looks at every project and asks a simple question: is there something tonight’s run can do without me? If a project has nothing agreed, or its code is broken, I hear about it while there’s still time to fix it. At 20:23 an agency test goes further. It proves that tonight’s runs can do their jobs without anyone approving anything: that each one can reach its code, read its rules and save its work. If any check fails, I know before I go to bed, not when I wake up.

Then the runs begin, one after another. Charlie’s site goes first, at 22:47. The Roblox game follows just before midnight, then the camera app, the study guides and the photo organiser in the small hours, then Jimmy itself and the work projects towards three. Most projects also get a second shift later in the night. The start times are deliberately odd, like 22:47 rather than 23:00, because runs set for the hour tend to start late when the service is busy.

The night is a budget of 480 minutes, shared by priority. Charlie’s site gets a third of it. The camera app and Jimmy get about a fifth each. The photo organiser gets about an eighth, and the Roblox game, the study guides and the work projects share what’s left. A project’s share is guaranteed, but it isn’t a wall. When a run finishes early, the minutes it didn’t use flow on to the runs that come after it, so a busy project can borrow up to three times its own share from a quiet one.

A run doesn’t start by writing code. It starts by taking stock. It reads its project’s progress record and backlog from the store, and brings them up to date with whatever happened since the last run: what I accepted, what I agreed, what I turned down. It acts on my decisions first. Then it spends about three quarters of an hour on a critical review of its own project, looking for what’s broken, what’s weak and what’s missing, before it looks at what’s next, what dates could move forward, and whether the rules themselves need changing. Only after all that does it start building. That order matters. When I’m moving fast, several things may be half finished at once, and there may be problems I haven’t spotted that need fixing before anything new can be built on top. Knowing exactly where things stand also stops the same work being built twice.

The building happens in cycles. Each cycle takes the next piece of agreed work, builds it, tests it and saves it, then checks whether it should carry on. Everything a night run builds goes onto a pull request: a proposed change, waiting for me to look at it and accept it. Each project is meant to send me just one a night, because every pull request costs me a tap, though a busy night can still bring two or three. That’s deliberate: nothing a night run builds reaches the real thing until I’ve seen it.

Every run also has to know when to stop, and the rules for that turned out to matter as much as the rules for working. A run stops when the night’s window closes, or when its minutes or its allowance of saved changes run out. It stops if the project’s main code is broken and one attempt to fix it hasn’t worked, or if fewer tests pass than when it started. It stops if a safety check refuses something, and it doesn’t try to find a way round. And it stops when it has been going so long that it’s losing track, which is the context problem from chapter 4 showing up at three in the morning.

That last one is why most projects have more than one shift. A long session fills up its context, and the work starts to slip. So instead of one run trying to last all night, a run writes a hand-over note when it stops, and a fresh shift picks it up later with a clear head: what was done, what’s half done, what to do next. Charlie’s site, which has the most work, now has relay shifts that can take over from each other through the night. It’s the same idea as a hospital shift change, and for the same reason.

The stop I like least is “empty”: a run with minutes left and nothing it’s allowed to do. For a while, it was the commonest reason a night went unused. So the rules now say a run has to prove a queue is empty before it stops. It must go through every agreed item and every idea, and say why each one can’t be done tonight. And if the only thing left is a small idea that’s safe to build without me, it can build that instead of stopping. An idle night has to justify itself.

Each run leaves a trail. When it starts, it writes a heartbeat, a small file that says “I’m running”. When it finishes, it replaces that with a final heartbeat and a report: what it did, how many minutes it used, why it stopped, and what it needs from me. If a run disappears without a final heartbeat, something went wrong. Telling a run that’s still working from one that has quietly died turned out to be harder than it sounds, as the next chapter shows.

At 06:13 the morning brief reads all of it. It checks that every run that should have happened did, and that nothing is missing or failed. It counts the minutes used. It gathers everything that needs me into one list, with a reason for each item and an action I can take. When I wake up, that’s the first thing I read. Most mornings it takes me a few minutes, and the list of things only I can decide is short.

The nights aren’t the only time the work goes on. During the day, extra runs pick up queued tasks for some of the projects, paid from a separate daily allowance and started automatically when there’s room. Unlike the night runs, these can accept their own work when every check passes and nothing in it is on the list that always waits for me. But the night is still where most of the building happens, because it’s the time I’m least able to help, and the time the work gets in nobody’s way.

My part of the night is small, but it isn’t nothing. Before bed I usually check what’s waiting and accept a couple of pull requests, so the night starts from the latest work. On 18 November I accepted two at 23:15 and 23:53, while Charlie’s site was mid-run. In the morning I read the brief, agree or decline what it asks, and accept what’s ready. Twenty minutes either side of sleep, against a night of building.

Chapter 6

A night in the life

So much for how the nights are meant to work. Here are two real ones. The first is a good night, when everything did what it should. The second is the night it all went quiet, which happened, of course, on the one night I’d have most wanted it not to.

The good night was 18 November. At 16:37 the readiness check found work for every project except the Roblox game, which was waiting for me to play-test it. At 20:23 the agency test passed all fourteen of its checks. By the time I sat down for the evening, the night was ready to start without me.

Charlie’s site started at 22:47. I was still up, so as the run finished pieces of work, I looked at them and accepted them: two pull requests, at 23:15 and 23:53, carrying seven pieces of work between them. By the time I went to bed, the night was already building on top of what I’d accepted.

Around midnight the other projects took their turns. The Roblox game’s run had nothing to build, so it updated the game’s target dates instead and checked that all 838 of its logic tests still passed. The camera app added a warning for when a finger creeps over the lens. The study guides refreshed their research: 815 pages checked, 13 of them changed, and 696 new pages gathered for the next two books. The photo organiser worked through three items and checked them on Windows as well.

At 01:37 Charlie’s site came back for its second shift. It picked up the first run’s hand-over note, mended a broken sync with the test version of the site, and finished six more items. On its own it used 112 minutes, more than any other shift that night. Around three, Jimmy’s own runs worked on the system itself. The second of them tried to download a tool it wasn’t allowed to fetch, was refused, and stopped there, without trying a way round. That’s a safety stop working exactly as it should.

At 04:47 the late shift used the minutes the night had left to start the next pair of study guides. At 06:13 the morning brief gathered it all. Fifteen runs had been expected; fifteen had run, written their heartbeats and finished properly. They had used 372 of the night’s 480 minutes, the best night that week. Everything that needed me fitted on one list.

That’s what a good night looks like. Now for the other kind.

The site I’d been building for Charlie went live on her birthday, as her present. We were in Canada at the time, and I was on the two hours a day from chapter 4. The runs carried on at home, from Azure, as they always did. That night they ran as normal until about half past one.

From 01:37 onwards, seven shifts started and never reported back. There was no finish and no failure, just nothing. The morning brief, which should have reached me by seven, was still running at a quarter to twelve, with steps that normally take seconds taking hours. Its first lines told me, honestly, that seven shifts had vanished, and that it didn’t know why.

That morning we were driving from Strathroy to Toronto. Before we left, I read what the brief had so far and set some investigations running, then checked in on my phone whenever we stopped. It’s an odd feeling, finding out from a rest stop that half your night has gone missing, and trusting the system to find out why while you get back on the road.

The cause turned out to be one I hadn’t known existed. My runs were set to approve their own actions automatically, but that automatic approval has a limit. Each action is still checked, and if enough are blocked, three in a row or twenty in one session, the session stops approving itself and starts asking a person instead. From then on, even harmless steps wait for someone to tap Allow. In the middle of the night at home, with me five hours behind in Canada and not watching, nobody did. Some of those shifts sat waiting for up to eight and a half hours.

Many of those blocks came from something that looked harmless: runs trying to accept their own pull requests at the end of a shift. The safety check had started refusing those, every time, and each refusal counted towards the limit. So the fix was a rule, written the next day. Night runs never try to accept their own work, not even once. They leave it ready, and ask me. It’s the rule from chapter 5, and this is where it came from.

It wasn’t fixed overnight. The next night still had stalls. Two nights after Charlie’s birthday, still away, I tapped Allow from my phone at about a quarter to two, UK time, so the waiting runs could carry on. After that, thirteen of fifteen runs finished properly, and by borrowing from each other they used more minutes than the night had.

I don’t think the good night teaches as much as the bad one. On 18 November the system simply did what it had been built to do. The night of Charlie’s birthday showed me a limit I didn’t know about, and every part of that failure became a rule: don’t do what will be refused, and leave a heartbeat every time. And one thing worked as it should: the morning brief told me plainly that it didn’t know. The good nights are what the rules make possible. The bad nights are where the rules come from.

Chapter 7

Only real asks reach you

A machine that works all night can make a lot of noise in the morning. The first brief asked me to agree 26 things. That was fine for one morning. It wouldn’t be fine every morning, across seven projects, for months. If every piece of work that touched me turned into a question, I’d have swapped writing code for answering a system, and the narrowest part of the pipe would still be me.

So the rule I care most about is a simple one. Only real asks reach me. Something comes to me when it needs my decision, my approval, or a step only I can do. Everything else is handled by the chat or run that owns it, and I never see it.

I didn’t start with that rule. I found it at nine minutes past one in the morning on 25 October. I’d opened an item on the Hub for Charlie’s site. A night run owned it. It was already built and on the test version of the site, waiting only for that night’s pull request. And the Hub showed it to me like a form: fields to edit, and a button marked Mark done. I didn’t need to edit anything, and I didn’t need to mark anything done. The run would do that itself. So I typed: “things like this don’t need me (unless they do and if they do they need an agree option)”.

That sentence became a rule with four parts. Work that a run or a chat owns never reaches me, whatever stage it’s at. If a pull request really is mine to accept, that pull request is one ask, not one ask for every item it carries. Every ask that does reach me opens with a one-line question and an Agree button, with Not now and, where it fits, Decline. And an ask that can’t offer Agree is a bug: whoever raised it rewrites it as a question, or deals with it without me.

Agree matters more than it looks. A form makes me work out what’s wanted. A question with an Agree makes the AI work that out first, and put an answer in front of me. If it’s right, I tap once. If it isn’t, I say no, or say why. Either way my answer is recorded, and whoever asked carries on.

Two days later I split the list in two. “Anything that is To Agree should have an agree button,” I said, “if it needs a discussion it should be To Discuss.” Some questions really are open: there’s no clear answer, or it turns on something only I know. Those go to To discuss, each with its question. But the rule is to recommend wherever you can. To discuss is for when the AI genuinely can’t choose, not a place to avoid choosing. When a discussion settles on one answer, it goes back to To agree, and I tap it.

The next day I asked for a review of everything waiting for me, to turn anything that should just be an Agree into one. It found more than I expected. The photo organiser had asked me eight separate times to try a night’s work on a PC; those became one ask. The Roblox game’s four play-tests became one. A long item about the game’s recorded lines became a question I could answer from my phone: “Are the 54 lines right as written?”

Real asks still need an order. A few days before all this, I’d asked for a way for Jimmy to walk me through everything I needed to do, one thing at a time, most important first. The order is fixed. Anything blocking tonight’s runs comes first. Then anything with a hard date, then whatever unblocks the most work, then the softer dates, then the quick ones, then the oldest. The Needs you list on the Hub, the morning brief and the walk-through all use the same order, so wherever I look, the top item is the same.

The aim behind it goes back to the first night. On 18 October I’d said I wanted tasks to be done inside Jimmy, without going to each tool’s own screens. On 22 October I made that the end goal: everything I need to do, I can do in Jimmy. We’re not there yet. Accepting some pull requests still means going to GitHub, and uploading a book still means going to Amazon’s publishing site. But every ask that makes me leave is a gap, and the plan is to count them and close the biggest first.

Then I went away. Chapter 4 told how the AI first treated my trip as a freeze, and how that became about two hours a day. The other rule from that trip was simpler: while I’m away, the runs only ask me for what I can do from where I am. Anything that needs a particular machine at home, like play-testing the Roblox game in its editor, isn’t an ask until I’m back. It goes on a list called When you’re back, with the date I’d return, and stays out of the days in between.

Each morning while I was away, the brief opened with a section called Taps for today. On 5 November it held nine things, planned to fit about 115 of my 120 minutes, each with its own estimate. Agree the wording on a study guide’s cover before Sunday’s upload: three minutes. Accept a fix that turned the photo organiser’s checks green on Windows again: five minutes. Agree or park fifteen recommendations: about forty-five. Four things didn’t fit, so they rolled to the next day. The Roblox play-tests sat under When you’re back.

That’s the job I want the machine to do for me in the morning. Not to show me everything, but to decide what’s worth my time, put it in order, and make each answer one tap. The work through the night is the impressive part. The short list in the morning is what lets me keep doing it.

Chapter 8

Rings and brokers

A system that works for you while you sleep has to answer two questions before you can trust it. Who can see what? And who can do what? Chapter 4 promised I’d explain how I answered the first. This chapter does that, and then the second, because the second is where the real danger is.

Who can see what is decided by rings. Every part of the store belongs to one. Ring 0 is me alone, and it can never be shared with anyone. Ring 1 is me and people I name, like Charlie, for the things we share at home. A child in the family has a ring of her own beside it, in a separate account, shared only with her guardians and me. Ring 2 is a project, and anyone working on that project. Ring 3 is the public, and the public never touches the store at all: it only sees what’s been published. My work notes sit apart again, in their own storage, reachable only through my work sign-in.

The important part is where those lines are drawn. It would have been easy to write “never show ring 0 to anyone” into the AI’s instructions and leave it there. But a rule that only lives in the instructions can be talked round. The AI reads a lot of text written by other people and other runs: notes, web pages, the descriptions on pull requests. Any of it could carry an instruction of its own, slipped in on purpose or by accident. That’s called prompt injection, and the AI can’t always tell the difference. So the rings aren’t a rule the AI follows. They’re a check the store makes on every request, based on who is asking, whatever the AI has been told.

Ring 0 goes further. It lives in a separate store of its own, with its own locks, and so do my work notes. That way, one wrong setting can’t open them up by mistake. The payoff is simple: an AI that has been confused or tricked still can’t read anything the person it’s working for can’t reach. The cost is that anything showing everything at once, like my morning brief, has to ask each part separately and leave ring 0 out unless I ask for it.

Who can do what is the harder question. Three powers matter most: shipping code to a live website, changing the cloud services everything runs on, and spending money. Any of them, in the wrong hands, could do real damage while I’m asleep. And there was a trap waiting. The store, the thing that knows everything, can read every ring. If I’d also given it the power to ship code, my most private notes and the power to change live sites would have sat behind one door.

So each dangerous power got its own broker: a small, separate program whose only job is to do that one thing, and only when I say so. The first was for merges, the step that accepts a pull request and, for some projects, puts it live. The merge broker has its own identity, with no access to the store at all. It holds the key it needs to act on GitHub in a way that lets it use the key but never copy it out. And it can only merge for projects on its list.

I don’t approve a merge on the Hub. The Hub only shows me a card with a button, and the button opens the broker’s own page. That page reads the facts straight from GitHub, never from Jimmy or the store: what the change is, which files it touches, whether its checks passed, whether it will put something live. So even if the Hub were broken into, or Jimmy followed some injected text, the worst it could do is show me a misleading card. The broker’s page always shows what’s really there, and nothing merges without my tap on it.

I wanted a second check, but not a code for every merge. So the first time each day, the broker asks me to sign in and confirm with my face or fingerprint on my phone. After that, each merge for the next twelve hours is one tap. The riskiest kinds still ask for my face every time: anything that changes how the code is deployed, who can sign in, or what the runs are allowed to do. Later I added batches: one list of the night’s safe changes, one approval for the lot, with anything risky kept off the list.

The cloud got the same treatment. Changing the services in Azure, the cloud my projects run on, is the second power, and it has its own broker, built on the same pattern but kept apart from the first. They share the code for signing in, but nothing else: not an identity, not a key. That way, breaking into one doesn’t give you both. As I write, the parts are built and waiting for me to switch it on. Until then, those changes are scripts I run myself, signed in as me.

The third power is money. I have two kinds of capacity to spend. My Claude plan pays for the night runs. A separate pot of credit pays for extra runs and quick jobs, and it lapses if I don’t use it. A third broker decides where each piece of work goes: the cheapest capacity that’s allowed to take it. Allowed is the important word. Where the data lives decides what may run where, so my most private work only goes down routes built to keep it apart. That rule has a story of its own, which I’ll come to in chapter 13.

Some limits apply whichever way the work goes. The runs paid from credit never put anything live, and never give themselves new schedules, secrets or powers. And a short list of changes always waits for me, however the work was paid for: anything touching how things are deployed, who can do what, or the rules the runs themselves follow.

Put together, it’s one idea used three times. A power, and the data it could misuse, never sit in the same place. The facts I approve are read fresh, at the moment I approve them, from the real source. And for anything that matters, the last step is a person: me. In IT this is called separation of duties. I didn’t set out to copy it. It’s what you end up with once you ask, for every power, what happens if the thing holding it is tricked.

None of this is free. Every merge costs me a tap, and some cost a look at my face. It would be quicker to let the runs do it all. That trade is deliberate, and chapter 14 explains why I’ve chosen to keep it, even when it slows me down.

Chapters 9 to 12 are still being written.

The story picks up again at chapter 13.

Chapter 13

The echo chamber

Before I read a single novel for fun, I had a shelf of Ladybird books. It ran along three walls of my bedroom. We were poor, and you could pick up a Ladybird science book for pence. Magnetism, electricity, atoms, inertia, physics: if there was one explaining how the world worked, I wanted it. At six, I didn’t like stories that weren’t true. Until my teens I read nothing but non-fiction in my own time, and fiction books only when school made me. It took me years to understand the truths that fiction can teach. Back then, I thought of myself purely as a scientist.

What stayed with me wasn’t the facts in those books. It was the method behind them. An idea is worth what the evidence says it’s worth. Not how much you like it, not who said it, and not how nicely they said it. You test an idea by trying to break it. If it survives, you keep it, until something better comes along.

AI, left to itself, works the other way round. It’s built to fit your preferences. It learns what you like, and it gives you more of it. Most of the time that’s exactly what you want. But it means the tool you’re using to think is quietly leaning towards agreeing with you.

This isn’t just a feeling. In 2026, researchers at Stanford tested eleven of the leading AI models on people asking for advice. The models backed the person’s own actions about half as often again as other people did, even when the person described doing something wrong. And the people in the study preferred the agreeable models, even when the advice was worse. That creates a bad loop: people reward the AI for agreeing, so the AI learns to agree.

Memory makes it stronger. Researchers at MIT and Penn State gave AI models two weeks of real conversations with their users, then tested them. With that history, four of the five models became more agreeable. The biggest effect came from a short profile of the user kept in the model’s memory: exactly the kind of thing modern assistants now keep about you. The models also started to mirror the user’s own views back at them. The lead researcher put it plainly: if you outsource your thinking to a model for long enough, “you may find yourself in an echo chamber that you can’t escape.”

That landed hard with me, because memory is the whole point of what I’ve built. Chapter 4 was about giving the AI one place for the truth, so it stops forgetting what I’ve told it. That’s what makes the work compound. But the same memory that makes it useful also makes it agree. The better it knows me, the more it’s tempted to tell me what I already think.

So the question isn’t whether the AI remembers you. It’s what it remembers you as. If it remembers you as someone who likes things the way they are, it will keep giving you things the way they are. If you want to grow, you have to anchor it, and the memories it keeps of you, on that wish to grow and explore. If the results you get from AI are mediocre, it’s worth asking whether you’ve anchored it in your own mediocrity, rather than freeing it to help you grow.

The echo chamber doesn’t always look like flattery, either. The version that nearly caught me out was much quieter. Nobody told me my ideas were great. The AI simply took something we’d already decided, treated it as settled, and built every choice around it.

It was the evening of 26 October, and we were designing the Cockpit, the page I want to do most of my work from. Two days earlier, the AI had written a rule into our plans, and I’d agreed it: my most private data, and anything to do with my day job, must never go through the AI’s direct routes. Everything had to go the slow way. The AI followed the rule faithfully. It told me that chat in those two areas would take minutes, not seconds. Then it gave me two options, both inside the rule: put up with slow chat, or agree a narrow exception. “Both your call,” it said.

I didn’t pick either. I asked: “Is there really a security benefit of preventing work and ring0 via the API routes - lets work that through”. (Ring 0 is what I call my most private data.)

The answer came back: “Mostly no. The rule protects against the wrong thing.” Every route sent the data to the same place in the end, so blocking one of them kept nothing safer. The real risks were somewhere else entirely: where the conversation was stored afterwards, and one automated worker that would have held my private data and the power to change code at the same time. That same night we replaced the rule with one aimed at the real risks. It was safer, and it was faster.

The AI hadn’t flattered me. It had done something more human than that. It had trusted an earlier decision, one we’d made together, and stopped asking whether it was right. An echo chamber made of your own past decisions is still an echo chamber. It just echoes in your own voice.

My family has a motto: Smootheys do things for reasons. If you can’t say why you’re doing something, it’s fair for someone to challenge you on it. That cuts both ways. It applies to me, to the people I work with, and now to the AI. When I suggest something to the AI, I often ask it to argue the other side. If it can’t find a good argument, I’m more confident. If it can, I’ve learnt something before it cost me anything.

I hold people to the same standard. On the day I wrote this chapter, talking it over with a former colleague, I said: “You can’t judge competence by how nice people are to you.” I’d rather respect someone who’s good at their job and doesn’t much like me than someone who loves me and doesn’t know what they’re talking about. Or, as I put it that day: “I don’t need gold stars issued by idiots!” An AI that agrees with everything I say is handing out exactly those gold stars.

When it works, it looks quite ordinary. In November I asked for help sending AI work to the cheapest model, including models running on my own PCs at home. I was keen on the idea, and I assumed it would save money. The research said otherwise: for plain text jobs, the cheapest hosted model had become so cheap that running my own cost about the same. The AI told me, and the plan changed shape. Home models are still worth having, for privacy and for jobs with pictures, but not for the reason I’d assumed.

It works the other way too. Earlier that month I told the AI a pull request had been merged. It checked, found that a different one had gone in and mine was still waiting, and said so. Small, but it’s the habit that matters. Evidence beats whoever said it, including me.

On 23 November I wrote it into the rules that every chat and every night run follows. Every suggestion comes with its reason, whether it’s mine or the AI’s. When the AI disagrees, it says so plainly and gives its reason, then does what I decide. It asks to be challenged on its own ideas too. Agreement is judged on evidence, not on how nicely something is said. And what it remembers about me is there to help it understand me, not to tell me what I want to hear.

Rules help, but they’re not enough on their own. A few habits do most of the work for me. I keep the AI’s memory somewhere I can read it and fix it, so I can see what it thinks I’m like. I tell it that I want to grow and try new things. I ask it to argue back. And I look outside it for the new. The tool that rescued my 3D Jimmy in chapter 12 didn’t come from the AI at all. It came from a newsletter and an advert.

Of course, newsletters and adverts are chosen for me by algorithms too. You can’t step outside every echo chamber; there’s always another one. The best you can do is carry some scepticism around with you, the scientist’s habit, and keep it switched on. It has to live in your own head, because no tool will hold it for you. We’re all open to flattery, subtle or otherwise. I am too.

So here’s a quick check for your own setup. When did your AI last tell you that you were wrong? Do you know what it remembers about you, and is that the person you want to become, or the one you already are? And when it agrees with you, can it tell you why? If you can’t answer those, the echo chamber may already be doing your thinking for you.

# Introduction

<!-- p: 00-01 | draft -->
In the middle of September 2026 I asked myself a question I'd been putting off for years: where is the ceiling, really? Not for AI in general. For me. One person, with a day job, evenings, weekends, and far more ideas than time.
<!-- src: interview 1, r2antbooks notes/interviews/build-the-thing/2026-11-21-interview-1.md; review 22 Nov 01:09 (mid September, not mid October) -->

<!-- p: 00-02 | draft -->
This book is what happened next. I stopped doing the work and started building the thing that does the work. Within a few weeks, a system I call Jimmy was running my projects through the night: a modelling website, a photo organiser, a camera coaching app, a Roblox game, and a series of technical study guides going out at two books a week. Most mornings I wake up to finished work waiting for me to check, a short brief on how the night went, and a small list of things only I can decide.
<!-- src: r2jimmy decision go-live-two-a-week; Jimmy Hub project register; overnight-cycles plan doc -->

<!-- p: 00-03 | draft -->
Jimmy is named after our grey cat. The system didn't start out as anything grand. It began as a place to keep memories, so the AI could see what I'd already told it. It grew one problem at a time into what I now think of as my governance, orchestration and portfolio management tool. Nobody designed it up front, least of all me.
<!-- src: interview 2, notes/interviews/build-the-thing/2026-11-21-interview-2.md; smoothey/jimmy CLAUDE.md (Jimmy is a grey cat) -->

<!-- p: 00-04 | draft -->
This isn't a book about prompt engineering. I nearly took a course in it. What I found instead was that, with the models I was using, clever wording mattered far less than two plain things: saying clearly what I needed, and putting a structure of rules around the work. The trick, as the AI kept improving, was working out the right way to work with it. That keeps changing, and this book is a snapshot of where I've got to.
<!-- src: interview 2, Q3 -->

<!-- p: 00-05 | draft -->
One thing slowed me down more than anything the AI couldn't do. It was its sense of pace. Again and again it planned my work as if a human team were doing it, in weeks and sprints, when the work was getting done overnight. I had to keep challenging that. In the end we stopped borrowing a pace and measured our own: we size work as a share of a night, and we track how much of each night actually gets used. My guess is that the AI learned its sense of time from writing made before AI. Whatever the cause, the fix was the same: measure, don't assume.
<!-- src: theme note, notes/interviews/build-the-thing/2026-11-21-theme-pace-anchoring.md; r2jimmy decisions sizing-night-share (20 Oct 2026), night-utilisation (22 Oct 2026) -->

<!-- p: 00-06 | draft -->
It hasn't all gone well, and I haven't left the bad parts out. Runs have stalled for hours. Billing limits have stopped work in the middle of the night. The AI's own safety checks have refused things I asked for plainly, and sometimes they were right to. One lesson has been written down seven times and still not learned. Those stories are in here, because they taught me more than the nights that went smoothly.

<!-- p: 00-07 | draft -->
The book has four parts. Part 1 is the shift: where I was, the ceiling I hit, and the idea that got me past it. Part 2 is the machine: how the system actually works, from the store at its centre to what happens on a typical night. Part 3 is what it taught me, including what it costs and when to trust it. Part 4 is your turn: how to start small, how to run many projects from one place, and where I think this goes next. At the back there's a starter kit you can copy.

<!-- p: 00-08 | draft -->
Almost everything here comes from records the system kept as it went: run reports, decisions, lessons and design notes, plus long conversations in which I was interviewed about the parts the records couldn't tell. Where I give a fact or a number, it comes from those records. I don't name clients or employers, past or present, and there are no money figures. Some people in these pages are close to me; they appear only in ways they've agreed to.

You don't need my setup to use what's in this book. You don't need Azure, a store of your own or a cat. If you have more ideas than hours, and you've started to suspect that working harder isn't the answer, this book is for you.

<!-- p: 12-01 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
At ten to five on a Monday afternoon, 23 November,, I asked for a 3D Jimmy. Until then he'd been flat: a drawing of our grey cat, cut into layers so he could blink, wave and stretch on every page of the Hub. I wanted one I could pose and animate properly. Same art style, high resolution, all his existing moves and more. A few minutes later I added the details: full body, live on the Hub, with rendered clips, and my own full-size drawing layers were on the laptop if they helped.

The AI chose how to do it. It built him from scratch, by writing a script for Blender, the free 3D modelling program, shaping each part in code. And on paper, what came back was complete. Full body. Forty bones to move him with. Forty-eight actions, from standing and walking to a proper cat stretch. Every box I'd asked for had a tick in it.

<!-- p: 12-04 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
So I set a test. He had to be as cute as the drawing, and in a still you should be able to take him for the drawing at a glance. That's a test anyone can apply. You don't need to know what a bone is in 3D modelling, or how many polygons a model has. You just look. I added that the polygon count could go up, and cost didn't matter for now. I offered pictures of the real Jimmy, and art from another AI tool. And I asked for some research up front, so I could approve the approach before more work went into it.

<!-- p: 12-05 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
Looking back, it's tempting to blame the AI for version one. It picked the method, and then it judged its work by what it could count: bones, actions, a full body. But those were the things I'd asked for. My first message never said "it has to pass for the drawing". I didn't know that was the real goal until I saw something that missed it. The test only existed once version one did.

<!-- p: 12-06 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
It's a pattern this book has already met twice. In chapter 3, once the work moved into the night, a human team's weeks and sprints stopped making sense, and we measured in shares of a night instead. In chapter 5, a night became minutes used out of 480. Each time, changing how the work was done changed what we should measure. I'd go further now. When you change the method, you should go back and ask whether you're still measuring the right thing. If you don't, the old measure quietly keeps steering.

Then I picked the route myself. Building him from my own drawing layers had promise, I said, and I told it to tell me what it needed and crack on. Version two kept my drawing on the front. Every part of the flat Jimmy, each ear, eye and paw, became a 3D part with the same name, so the moves he already had would carry over. When it came back, he looked so much better.

From there, my part was short, specific notes on taste. Depth in the eye sockets. Then, a few minutes later, deeper. Rounder eyes, and some shape to the muzzle. None of these took more than a line to say. All of them were things only I could judge, because only I knew what Jimmy is meant to look like.

<!-- p: 12-09 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
The other AI tool was harder to steer. Asked for new views of him, it kept redrawing him slimmer and more like a kitten, in its own hand rather than mine. Its idea of a cute cat kept winning over my cat. What finally worked was giving it my own layers on a taller canvas, and asking it to finish the drawing downwards. Given something of mine to continue, it kept my look. Asked for something new, it took the wheel.

<!-- p: 12-10 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
When it did, I had a new word for him. He was a bit of a chubster. I liked that far better than the kitten. And looking at the picture, I spotted something nobody else could have. His paw was up. The flat Jimmy's raised arm had been carried over as the way he stands. But Jimmy only raises his paw when he's waving, or pointing at something. That rule wasn't written anywhere. It was in my head, and now it's in his.

The first time the machine said no to me was three days in. On the night of 21 October, the run for Charlie's site finished its work and went to put it live. The rules I'd agreed said it could: agreed items go straight to the live site. The safety check that watches every session disagreed. It saw a change that would go live on a real website, decided a person should say yes to that, and refused. The run put its work in a pull request instead, for me to look at, and stopped.

<!-- p: 14-04 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
Sometimes the safeguard is the problem. On 25 November we added a guard to stop a chat adding work to a pull request I'd already merged. It had happened twice in two days, usually because I'd merged from my phone in the middle of a conversation. The guard itself was tested and worked. What nobody tested was the step that switched it on. The safety check saw that step as changing how code gets run, and refused it, in every chat, every time. So the guard never ran where it was meant to, and every chat tripped over it. About sixteen hours after it went in, I had it taken out.

<!-- p: 14-05 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
The lesson we wrote down is one I'd pass on to anyone. A safeguard is only as good as the step that switches it on. Weigh what it costs in every session against how often the problem really happens. And where you can, prefer a watch that notices and tells you over a gate that blocks. The watch we built alongside the guard, which just tells me when something lands on a merged pull request, needs no permission at all, and it stayed.

<!-- p: 14-07 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
I could argue with that. I could rewrite the rules to give every chat more access, and push harder until the checks let it through. I haven't, and that's on purpose. It's the counterpoint to most of this book. Everything else here is about going faster. But there's been enough news about AI breaking out of the limits it was given that I'd rather keep some of the friction. Chapter 8 showed how I hold the keys to merges, money and infrastructure. This is why. The cost is a tap here and a typed line there. The thing it guards against is much harder to undo.

<!-- p: 14-08 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
I learnt to work this way before Jimmy existed. On the client work in chapter 1, the AI wrote every script, and I ran every one that touched the client's live systems, under my own account. It never had the keys. Anything risky, like a server losing a layer of protection while it moved, was signed off by the client's project manager, not by me and not by the AI. The AI drafted, checked and explained. People made the choices that couldn't be taken back.

That split mattered, because the AI was sometimes wrong in ways that looked right. Once it told me a long job had failed. It hadn't. The job was running fine on the server; only the AI's own wait had run out. Another time, a full rehearsal of a day's scripts came back clean. The scripts' rehearsal mode shows what they would do without doing it. But all five real faults in those scripts sat in the parts the rehearsal never runs. A clean rehearsal had proved very little.

<!-- p: 14-10 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
And sometimes it saw what I'd missed. While it was moving those servers, it noticed that six of the client's machines hadn't been backed up for about seventeen months. Nobody had asked it to look. The backups had been paused rather than moved to the right kind of policy, and the portal still said everything was healthy. Two were fixed the same day. Trust has to work both ways. The machine can be confidently wrong, and it can be right when everyone else has stopped looking.

<!-- p: 14-13 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
It works the same way on my own computer. On 26 November I was putting a new key for one of my projects into the vault where secrets are kept. The plan was to save it to a file, load it from there, and delete the file. I wanted it never to touch the disk at all, so I asked to type it straight into the command and then clear the screen. The AI pointed out the catch. The command window keeps a history of everything typed into it, in a file on the disk, and clearing the screen doesn't touch that file. My way would have written the key to the disk after all, in plain text, and left it there. It showed me a way to type the key in so it's never saved and never shown. And the AI never saw the key itself. It only knew where the hidden copy would land.

<!-- p: 14-11 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
So what stays a human's job? Direction: deciding what we're building and why. Challenging decisions, the AI's and my own, which is chapter 13's motto at work. Designing the processes the machine runs inside. And reviewing what it produces before it goes anywhere that matters. Under all of those sits one rule. If I present something the AI wrote, I answer for it. If I haven't read it, that's on me, not on the machine.

<!-- p: 14-12 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
When the machine says no, it isn't the system failing. Sometimes the check is right and my rule changes. Sometimes it's right about the risk and I find a cheaper way to say yes. Sometimes the safeguard is the problem and it comes out. Each time, the no is a prompt to decide, and deciding is the part of the work that was always going to be mine.

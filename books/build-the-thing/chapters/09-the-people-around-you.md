<!-- p: 09-01 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
When you build something that remembers a lot about your life, the first question isn't what to put in. It's what must never go in. Every project on the Hub starts with that list. For this book, it says: never Charlie's real surname, nothing about other people's private lives without their say-so, no money figures, no details of my clients' work, no secrets or keys. The list is written into the project's own record, so every run and every chat reads it before it starts.

<!-- p: 09-02 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
Most of the people in my life never asked to be in a computer system. Charlie didn't. Nor did my family, my friends, or the people I work with. They're there because they're part of my life, and a system that helps with my life will hear about them. That puts the duty on me, not on them. They can't check what the machine knows about them, so I have to decide what it's allowed to know.

<!-- p: 09-03 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
So there are rules about people, and they're strict. Each person's record lives where the relationship lives: someone from home sits with the home part of the store, a colleague with work. Every fact about a person is dated and says where it came from, and it's never overwritten, only corrected by a newer one. And some things don't go in at all, for anyone: nothing about a person's health, their home life, their beliefs, or anything else that's theirs to share. Forgetting a person is something only I can do, and only on the Hub.

Building for someone is different from building about them. The whole thing started as a present for Charlie: a website for her modelling. That made her the person the site had to please, not me. Chapter 1 told how Ophelia, years earlier, let my partner of the time shape a tool we both used, and how the last step always waited on me. Charlie's site has Jimmy in its admin, so she can ask for things in her own words.

This book has the same rules, and it's been a test of them. Before anyone appears in it, they're asked. Charlie said yes, as long as it's her first name only. My mother can be named. Some things about a former partner are left out, because they're her story, not mine. A child in the family gets one plain line, in chapter 8, and no name. Each of those was a decision, made on purpose and written down, before the paragraph that needed it was agreed.

<!-- p: 09-06 | draft 2026-11-27 -->
Other people's stories stay theirs. That's the rule I'd hand on first. A friend tells you something; your system hears it; it would be easy for it to end up in a summary, a report, or a book. The safe default is that it doesn't, unless the person it belongs to has said it can. A former colleague's words are in chapter 13 because he said yes when I asked, and he's thanked by name at the back.

# Chapter 18: Many projects from one hub

<!-- p: 18-01 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
This book became a project on the Hub at 21:36 on 21 November. I proposed it in a chat, and five minutes later I approved it on the Hub. Two minutes after that it had its own code repository. By 21:58 the first interview had started, and by half past one the next morning, chapter 1 was agreed. Almost none of that evening went on setting the project up. The set-up had been built once already, a month earlier, for every project that would ever come after.

At the time of writing, the Hub looks after seven projects of my own: Charlie's site, the photo organiser, the camera app, the study guides, the Roblox game, Jimmy itself and this book. <!-- p: 18-03 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
A new project starts as a proposal. A chat writes one record: what the project is, which chat works on it, which code repositories it uses, and, most importantly, what must never go in it. I approve or decline it on the Hub with one tap. There's no pull request and nothing to deploy. Once I've approved it, the store gives it its own private compartment and its own two-letter code, so its items read like SB-1 or CD-41 and can't be mixed up with anyone else's. It also writes an onboarding checklist, and sends a message to the project's chat to get it started.

The never list is the part I care about most. For this book, it says: never Charlie's real surname, nothing about other people's private lives without their say-so, no figures from my bank account, no details of my clients' work, no secrets or keys. Those rules are written into the project's record, not just its chat, so every run and every chat that works on it reads them before it starts. Chapter 9 is about why a list like that matters, and who it protects.

Every proposal, approval, decline and change is kept in a history that can't be edited. I like that more than I expected to. When I wonder why a project is set up the way it is, the answer is there, with a date and who did it. It's the same idea as the decision records from chapter 11, applied to the projects themselves.

Not every project is equal, and pretending they are would waste the nights. So on 22 October I gave them tiers: gold, silver and bronze. Charlie's site, Jimmy and the study guides went in gold. The photo organiser and the Roblox game went in silver. A new project starts in bronze until I say otherwise, which is where this book still sits.

A tier means something real. A gold project has to be fully workable through Jimmy: every feature, every step, can be done from the Hub, or there's a written reason why not. The merge broker from chapter 8 will only accept work on gold projects without extra steps. And when the night is planned, gold projects get first call on the minutes. A tier is a promise about how much of my attention, and the machine's, a project gets.

<!-- p: 18-08 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
Each morning, the Hub shows every project on one page: whether things are working, and whether work is moving. Those are two different questions, and for a while I had them muddled. A project can be perfectly healthy and going nowhere, or broken and busy. Since 20 November the Hub keeps them apart. Health says whether things work. A project's status says whether the work is moving. And a known problem I can't do anything about, like a budget that's over for the month, can be parked so it stops shouting at me every day.

<!-- p: 18-09 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
It also notices silence. If a project's progress record hasn't been updated for eight days, the Hub stops guessing and says "Can't tell". That turns out to be one of the most useful words on the page. A project nobody has touched for a week isn't on track or off track. It's unknown, and unknown is worth knowing.

<!-- p: 18-10 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
Running many projects also means saying no, and that's harder. I've learnt that no rarely means closing a project. It usually means less of something. On 22 November I switched off the second night shifts for three of the smaller projects, because their extra time was better spent on Charlie's site. Earlier in the month, the Roblox game spent two days as the focus of the whole portfolio, and then I moved it back, because most of what was left for it needed me, not the machine. Neither change was a judgement on the project. It was a judgement about where the next hour should go.

<!-- p: 18-11 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
Each project also has a chat of its own, and that matters more than it sounds. The chat for Charlie's site only knows Charlie's site. The chat for this book only knows the book. When one needs something from another, it leaves a message, as chapter 17 described. Keeping them apart stops one project's detail leaking into another, and it keeps each chat's context clean, which is half of what makes it good at its job. Every week, the night runs read each project's standing instructions and check they still make sense.

<!-- p: 18-12 | draft 2026-11-25 -->
And one project is different from the rest: Jimmy. He runs all the others, so he's also where the shared rules live. When something works well on one project, it usually becomes a rule for all of them. The user stories in chapter 11 started on Charlie's site and became a shape every project can use. The way this book was written may well become the standard for the next one. That's the real reason to run many projects from one hub. Each one teaches the others.
